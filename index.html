Cosmic Microwave Background

The cosmic microwave background (CMB), the "afterglow of the Big Bang," has been instrumental in in shaping our modern understanding of the universe and its evolution. The temperature and polarization of this ancient bath of radiation contain a wealth of statistical information that allows us to test our theories of how the universe came to be. The CMB's formation and anisotropies (as well as the physical mechanisms driving them) are well-understood, allowing for tight constraints on the contents and underlying parameters that describe our universe. Much of my work in this area concerns dealing with the foregrounds and lensing that obstruct our CMB observations at high resolutions, through analytical and computational techniques.

Scientific Communication

Beyond technical research, I’m interested in how scientific knowledge is written, shared, and shaped over time and how best to encourage better scientific communication. I've explored the structure and style of physics literature using stylometry, machine learning, and literary techniques to see how writing patterns, citations, and formality influence impact and accessibility. I've additionally served as student mentor for high school students in the NASA HUNCH program and editor-in-chief of SMU's Journal of Undergraduate Research to encourage high-quality and effective science writing and pursuits.

Quantum Information Science

Quantum information science explores how the probabilistic nature of quantum physics can be used to improve computing and communication. My work in this area focuses on using light-based (photonic) systems in the high-dimensional and continuous-variable quantum computing regimes. This includes the creation of novel circuits to generate high-quality random numbers, modeling quantum network architectures for secure communication and information processing, and developing tools for future modeling ventures beyond the classic qubit-based regime.
